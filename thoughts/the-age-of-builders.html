The golden age of builders

September 2026 · 5 min read

Growing up, I was always curious to learn new things, try new technologies, and experience new ways of doing things. The phrase “using your hands” to build never really sat well with me, perhaps because the only form of building I was exposed to at that age involved physical objects, mechanical tools, and machines. But destiny has a funny way of finding us.

I still remember this line from Jeff Goldblum’s character, Ian Malcolm, in Jurassic Park: “Life finds a way.” Similarly, I feel like life has a way of fulfilling its prophecy for the relentlessly curious. Or perhaps it is the relentlessly curious who have a tendency to find their own way to quench their thirst for knowledge, experimentation, and new experiences.

I vividly remember the moment, about five years ago, when I began to understand the power of software and its ability to reproduce, replicate, and scale at an overwhelmingly faster rate than anything I had encountered before. That was the moment I realized software was the medium I had been looking for, and that I needed to make it work somehow. And so it began: my journey into the world of software and the seemingly endless possibilities it offered.

Fast-forward to today, and AI has taken that idea to another level. Almost anyone now has access to incredibly fast, capable, and intelligent tools right at their fingertips. I genuinely believe this is the kind of technology people would have paid billions for 20 or 30 years ago, and we now have access to much of it for free. I couldn't be happier. My love for software has evolved once again, this time equipped with the tools of modern AI. The era of builders is here. There is no denying it.

What excites me most is that we now have incredibly capable tools that can help people build and iterate on software on demand. For people like me, this feels like a dream come true. Almost anyone with a computer or smartphone can now turn an idea into something tangible. I'm getting chills just typing that.

If you can imagine something, you can increasingly build it with AI. Yes, it really is becoming that simple.

But there is an important distinction: the ability to build does not automatically mean the ability to build well.

And I'm not talking about the slop that AI can spit out. I'm talking about well-defined, well-crafted, and well-presented artifacts, the things people intentionally create with AI because they know what they want and can recognize when something isn't right.

As Naval Ravikant put it, “vibe coding is more addictive than any video game ever made, if you know what you want to build.” That last part is the catch.

If you know what you want to build: that is the part that will increasingly separate those who can build from those who can build well. And the people who already possess that instinct for building, combined with curiosity, creativity, and taste, are going to have an incredible advantage.

When execution becomes cheaper, judgment becomes more important. Taste becomes the differentiator. When everyone can generate something, the differentiator becomes knowing what to generate, why to generate it, and what to do when the first version is wrong. The people who already have a natural curiosity for building, experimenting, and creating are going to have an incredible advantage, not necessarily because they know how to code better than everyone else, but because they know how to explore.

The good news is that this isn't a skill exclusive to a select few. It can be learned. Think of it as a muscle. The more you use these tools, the better you become at articulating what you want, recognizing what isn't right, understanding why it isn't right, and figuring out how to get closer to the result you envisioned. Every bad output becomes feedback. Every iteration teaches you something. Over time, you develop judgment.

Jensen Huang has made a similar point: AI itself may not replace everyone, but people who know how to use AI will have an advantage over those who don't. AI is a tool, and that tool becomes significantly more powerful when placed in the hands of someone with a clear end goal, strong judgment, and the ability to articulate what they want.

Andrej Karpathy has also pointed out that one of the most interesting programming languages today is English. There is something profound about that idea. If you can imagine it, and you have reliable Wi-Fi, you can increasingly build it.

But, like every technological shift, it takes repetition. When spreadsheets like Excel became widely available, people had to learn how to use them before they could take advantage of what they made possible. I think we're going through a similar transition with modern AI tools. People need to learn how to interact with them, adapt to their quirks, and repeatedly iterate until the output is good enough.

And perhaps that is only the beginning. Consumer AI may eventually reach an inflection point where much of this process becomes automated, and machines begin to outperform humans across an ever-expanding range of cognitive tasks. But until then, the ability to imagine, articulate, judge, and iterate remains deeply human and increasingly valuable.

No matter what ultimately happens, I don't think the times have ever been better for people who are inherently curious, or for those who genuinely want to experiment, explore new ideas, and build things that did not exist before. The golden age of builders has only just begun.
